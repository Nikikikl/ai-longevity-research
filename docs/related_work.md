# Related work and scientific positioning

This is a focused comparison of published studies that bear on the HAGR overlap, its interpretation, or a possible follow-up. Our project is an independent reproducible reanalysis and evidence review of two curated exports.

## Bonnefous et al. (2024)

Bonnefous and colleagues asked when aging-associated protein-coding gene families and protein interactions arose across five fungal and animal species. They used STRING v11.5 networks and OMA orthology data from November 2022 to estimate the earliest detectable ancestry of gene families and interactions. They compared age distributions and examined network centrality, age assortativity and ancestral networks. The human GenAge–CellAge overlap was one result within that evolutionary analysis. Our project instead documents how human identifiers were resolved, which direct GO Biological Process (BP) annotations the shared genes carry, and what the primary papers support for three genes. [Published article, DOI 10.1007/s11357-024-01234-9](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11357-024-01234-9), [full text, PMID 38862758](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11335996/)

### Release labels and eligibility

The published Methods assign Build 3 to GenAge and Build 20 to CellAge. HAGR identifies Build 20 as a GenAge release (2020-02-09, 307 human genes) and Build 3 as a CellAge release (2023-04-23, 866 manipulation-associated genes). The labels appear to have been transposed. GenAge Build 20 and CellAge Build 3 are the likely intended pair, but the article supplies no archived HAGR inputs or checksums that establish the exact files. Our project uses GenAge Human Build 21 (2023-08-28) and CellAge Build 3. The Build 21 notes report no membership changes outside the expression signature. [Published Methods, “Dataset assembly”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11335996/), [HAGR Build 20 announcement](https://www.genomics.senescence.info/news/), [GenAge Build 21 notes](https://genomics.senescence.info/genes/release.html), [CellAge Build 3 notes](https://genomics.senescence.info/cells/release.html)

The denominators represent different eligibility rules. Bonnefous et al. mapped protein-coding genes through Entrez and Ensembl identifiers to STRING proteins. Their phylostratigraphic analysis required a successful mapping to an OMA orthogroup of known origin and applied a low-sequence-complexity filter. We count confirmed current human NCBI Gene IDs with reciprocal HGNC mappings, without requiring a protein-network or orthology match for set membership. We exclude one conflicting CellAge record (FNTB / CHURC1-FNTB) from the 866 source IDs. The different requirements can account for lower exploitable counts. Without the published input files and a per-gene inclusion log, we cannot assign the four-gene and thirteen-gene differences to specific exclusions or quantify any effect of release differences. [Published Methods](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11335996/), [identifier audit](../reports/gene_identifier_audit.md)

### Can we verify the shared gene list?

The article points to its text and online supplements for data. The publisher lists 20 supplementary figures and three tables. We inspected the three table files. Table S1 gives hallmark-level counts from Open Genes and Aging Atlas. Table S2 summarizes inferred ancestral interactions. The final spreadsheet, listed as Table S3 on the publisher page, gives statistics for age-homogeneous network components. None supplies the 113 gene IDs or both eligible human input lists. We did not locate a separate deposit linked by the authors. [Publisher supplement index and data availability statement](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11357-024-01234-9)

The studies agree on **113 as a count**. Gene-by-gene equivalence remains unverified. A direct check would require the authors' overlap or both eligible lists in a documented namespace. Entrez IDs could be compared after version and identifier checks. Ensembl or STRING protein IDs would need a versioned crosswalk with ambiguous or unmapped IDs reported before comparing the sets. Our side of that comparison is available by Gene ID and HGNC ID, with original record ordinals. [Shared-gene table](../reports/gene_overlap_genes.csv)

## Contribution and limits

The numerical overlap was reported before. Our contribution is a traceable implementation for identified snapshots. The workflow verifies SHA-256 checksums, records reciprocal NCBI–HGNC decisions and explicit exclusions, and retains the CellAge ordinals for every shared gene. The GO table preserves individual GAF line numbers and reports sensitivity to excluding IEA. The TP53, CDKN2A and TERT review distinguishes a curated label from an intervention, assay and biological model. It also identifies repeated citations and annotations whose references do not directly establish the stated mechanism. This independent workflow uses the same HAGR resources and does not provide independent biological validation. [Report and reproduction instructions](../reports/research_report_v1.md), [primary-evidence review](../reports/gene_case_studies.md)

An exact rerun requires the original files. A rolling URL and a checksum cannot recover an earlier snapshot. For example, the official NCBI Gene URL reported a later Last-Modified date and a different file size when checked on 2026-11-08. Retain the pinned local files and verify their hashes before running the workflow. [Identifier reference manifest](../data/reference_provenance.json), [GO manifest](../data/go_provenance.json)

## Proposed Phase 7 | Replication across human fibroblast RNA-seq cohorts

**Question.** Are genes in the fixed 113-gene overlap more strongly associated with donor age in cultured human dermal fibroblasts than expression-matched genes outside the overlap, and do estimated directions replicate in a separate cohort?

Use the healthy-donor samples from Fleischer et al. (GSE113957) for discovery and the independent GESTALT cohort from Tsitsipatis et al. (GSE226189) for replication. Both studies profiled cultured dermal fibroblasts, so the design narrows the cell context while leaving cohort and culture differences to assess. Confirm metadata availability and donor independence before analysis. Restrict the primary comparison to the common adult age range. Freeze the gene list, identifier mapping, expressed-gene background and primary test before estimating effects. Fit donor-level age associations per decade, adjusting for available covariates such as sex, batch and culture passage. Use a gene-set test that accounts for correlated expression, and report effect sizes, uncertainty and direction concordance across cohorts. Treat gene-level and subgroup tests as secondary and correct for multiple testing. [Fleischer et al., DOI 10.1186/s13059-018-1599-6](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13059-018-1599-6), [GSE113957](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE113957), [Tsitsipatis et al., DOI 10.1111/acel.13915](https://doi.org/10.1111/acel.13915), [GSE226189](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE226189)

Age-associated RNA abundance would establish an association within this culture setting. It cannot establish senescent cell burden or the direction of a gene perturbation. If a reproducible association emerges, a separate prediction study could ask whether the fixed panel adds value over a baseline, using donor-level splits and a held-out cohort. Phase 6 proposes this design without fitting models or analyzing new RNA-seq data.
